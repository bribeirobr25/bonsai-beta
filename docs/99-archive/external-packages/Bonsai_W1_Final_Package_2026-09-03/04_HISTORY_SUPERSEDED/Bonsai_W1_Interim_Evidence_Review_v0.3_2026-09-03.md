## 1. Executive finding

The evidence no longer supports a simple story such as:

> “People do not want to track bonsai.”

Nor does it support:

> “People clearly want a better bonsai tracker, therefore build one.”

A sharper pattern is emerging:

> **Longitudinal record/history has real value for some growers, but the value
> must repeatedly outrun the maintenance cost, complexity, reminder noise,
> data-durability risk and the convenience of free workarounds.**

That is a **strategic inference**, not yet a validated market conclusion.

---

## 2. Theme A · Record/history value is real

### Literal evidence
Across Bonsai Album, Bonsai Care App, Appy Bonsai and public forum discussions,
users describe value in:
- remembering what was done and when;
- photo progression;
- collection organization;
- replacing spreadsheets / OneNote;
- preserving per-tree history;
- seeing development over years.

Bonsai Album reviews span many years, including beginners and collection-heavy
users. Appy Bonsai includes a reviewer self-identifying as a serious
40+ year bonsai artist who values detailed collection records.

### Interpretation
`SUPPORTED SIGNAL`

The job `remember / retrieve / see progression` exists across more than one
product generation and more than one experience segment.

### What it does NOT prove
- H1B prevalence
- retention distribution
- willingness to pay across the target market
- that Bonsai should build P1

---

## 3. Theme B · The real competitor is often “good enough” tooling

### Literal evidence
Growers publicly report using:
- Google Photos
- OneNote
- spreadsheets
- spiral notebooks
- index cards
- per-tree photo folders
- custom-built forms/tools

Some explicitly prefer these because they are flexible, familiar or
cross-platform.

### Interpretation
`OBSERVED`

The competitive set is not only bonsai apps.

### Strategic implication
A dedicated product cannot win merely by having a bonsai-specific schema.
It must create enough incremental value to justify migration and repeated
logging.

---

## 4. Theme C · Structure has a cost

### Literal evidence
Observed complaints include:
- too many fields;
- irrelevant required fields;
- forced photos;
- difficult image handling;
- long collection lists;
- inability to batch actions;
- cumbersome sync/transfer;
- too many clicks;
- need for custom categories or tags.

A Bonsai Album beginner estimated roughly 30 seconds for a per-tree journal
entry and asked for global/batch actions. Other users explicitly build their
own tools when standard apps do not fit.

### Interpretation
`SUPPORTED SIGNAL`

There is a recurring tension:

> **more structure → better retrieval and analysis, but higher capture friction**

### Strategic implication
The domain model can be rich internally while capture must remain progressive.
This is consistent with the canonical “capture first, structure progressively”
principle, but W1 does not validate that implementation.

---

## 5. Theme D · Reminders are valuable and dangerous

### Literal evidence
Users value reminders for:
- wire removal;
- repotting;
- fertilizer;
- seasonal work;
- remembering tasks across many trees.

But experienced feedback also warns:
- care should not be too date-driven;
- species/context can make generic reminders wrong;
- many trees can create notification overload;
- some users want only self-created rules.

### Interpretation
`SUPPORTED SIGNAL`

The job is less:
> “tell me the calendar”

and more:
> **“help me not forget what matters for this tree, without pretending dates are horticultural truth.”**

This is interpretation, not a product requirement.

---

## 6. Theme E · Data durability affects trust and purchase

### Literal evidence
- A long-term paid BonsaiDo user reacted strongly when trees/notes appeared lost.
- A prospective Bonsai Care buyer explicitly wanted to test export before paying
  and moving the full collection, in case the service was discontinued or the
  subscription ended.
- Bonsai Album users value backup/export but report sync friction.

### Interpretation
`SUPPORTED SIGNAL`

As the record accumulates, switching cost and fear of loss increase.

### Strategic implication
Data portability is not merely a power-user checkbox. It can influence whether
a user is willing to entrust years of history to a product.

This is **not** evidence that provenance is validated.

---

## 7. Theme F · Collection size changes the job

### Beginner
Observed needs lean toward:
- basic guidance;
- reassurance;
- reminders;
- species/timing context;
- easy initial logging.

### Expanding / large collection
Observed needs lean toward:
- search;
- folders/collections;
- batch actions;
- retrieval;
- flexible logging;
- export;
- cross-device access.

### Experienced
Observed signals include:
- strong value for historical records;
- custom rules;
- less tolerance for generic advice;
- interest in experiments/results;
- desire to preserve personal judgment rather than outsource it.

### Status
`SUPPORTED SIGNAL`

The current evidence argues against treating “bonsai grower” as a single
workflow.

---

## 8. Theme G · Advice adds an authority tax

### Literal evidence
The calibration already found:
- incorrect AI identification at a purchase moment;
- experienced users skeptical of generic timing;
- public comments that trees should be observed rather than managed purely by
  dates.

### Interpretation
`SUPPORTED SIGNAL`

A record can be wrong in usability.
Advice can be wrong in a way that harms a tree.

That asymmetry reinforces, rather than amends, the current advice-ladder
governance.

---

## 9. Theme H · Community value is narrower than “social feed”

### Literal evidence
Some users like seeing:
- other trees at different stages;
- progression;
- other growers’ work.

Other feedback shows privacy/default-sharing concern and no evidence yet that
a generic feed causes retention.

### Interpretation
- progression/social discovery value: `SUPPORTED SIGNAL`
- generic community feed as retention engine: `UNVALIDATED`

No evidence yet upgrades Tree-Led Relationships or Comparable Journey from
their current validation status.

## 10. Why dozens of apps may coexist without a clear winner

These are **working explanations**, not findings of causality.

### Explanation 1 · Heterogeneous jobs
Beginner guidance, collection management, historical journaling and expert
experimentation are materially different jobs.

### Explanation 2 · Good-enough substitutes
Google Photos, OneNote, notebooks and spreadsheets already solve enough of the
problem for some growers.

### Explanation 3 · Repeated-maintenance economics
The user pays logging cost every time they interact with a tree, while some
benefits arrive months or years later.

### Explanation 4 · Structure-friction tradeoff
A schema rich enough to be useful later can become tedious during capture.

### Explanation 5 · Reminder paradox
Reminders create immediate utility but become noise or false precision when
generic.

### Explanation 6 · Trust / durability
A multi-year record makes app death, subscription loss, bad sync and data loss
more consequential.

### Explanation 7 · Authority mismatch
Adding AI/care guidance can attract beginners but simultaneously create trust
risk and expert rejection.

**Current status for this combined explanation: `SUPPORTED SIGNAL`, causal
mechanism still UNVALIDATED.**

## 12. W2 handoff

### Evidence
`OBSERVED`
- explicit paid users exist;
- users report annual/monthly upgrades;
- some users run two paid bonsai apps in parallel;
- at least three Android products have 10K+ download brackets.

`UNVALIDATED`
- paid conversion;
- churn;
- LTV;
- category revenue;
- viable acquisition economics.

### Sharpened W2 question
Not:
> “Will anyone pay for bonsai software?”

But:
> **Which recurring value is strong enough to justify payment after the user
> already has a free photo folder, spreadsheet or notes app?**

## 13. W3 handoff

Repeated user language centers on:
- remember;
- history;
- progression;
- one place;
- my trees;
- what I did;
- photos;
- don’t lose it;
- don’t overwhelm me.

Trust claims around AI/advice carry materially higher downside than record/
memory claims.

---

## 14. W4 handoff

Potential acquisition moments now supported by user evidence:
- first tree;
- collection becomes too large for memory;
- spreadsheet/photo-folder frustration;
- forgotten task;
- desire to compare progression;
- need to preserve records before switching tools;
- purchase/identification uncertainty.

Objects that naturally travel:
- before/after;
- progression photo;
- shareable tree history.

A growth loop that requires heavy logging before first visible value remains
high-risk.

## 15. Open questions W1 reviews cannot resolve

1. What share of growers keep private longitudinal records?
2. How often does novelty decay into abandonment?
3. What exact capture burden is sustainable?
4. Does progression/history alone create recurring return?
5. Is export merely requested or actually used?
6. Does structured history improve transfer/provenance value?
7. Does Comparable Journey outperform direct advice?
8. Which segment is economically large enough?
9. Why do some 10K+ Android products convert to durable use and others not?
10. Are users switching between bonsai apps or simply trying many and reverting
    to general tools?

## 16. Next W1 gate

The next useful step is **not another competitor-count chase**.

Now that the original scan is demonstrably incomplete, W1 should:
1. freeze a refreshed discovery manifest;
2. run a fresh four-storefront measurement for the expanded Apple set;
3. mine Google Play reviews for the highest-signal products;
4. oversample explicit abandonment / switching / data-loss reviews;
5. code workarounds as competitors;
6. produce the first cross-product evidence matrix with denominators limited to
   the collected corpus, not the market.

The research question stays behavioural:
> **What makes a grower keep recording, and what makes them stop?**
